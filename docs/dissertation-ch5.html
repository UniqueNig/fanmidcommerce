Chapter Five

Summary, Conclusion and Recommendations

5.1 Introduction

This final chapter draws the study to a close. It begins by summarising what was done across the preceding chapters, then states the conclusions that can fairly be drawn from the work, sets out its specific contributions to knowledge and practice, acknowledges its limitations honestly, and offers recommendations for future development. The purpose is not to introduce new material but to step back and assess, in light of the aim and objectives stated in Chapter One, what the study has achieved and what it points towards next.

5.2 Summary of the Study

The study set out to enhance and thoroughly document FanMidCommerce, a full-stack e-commerce web application, in order to demonstrate the practical use of modern web technologies and to assess the platform's suitability for small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the Nigerian context. The work unfolded across the chapters as follows.

Chapter One established the problem. It showed that, although internet and mobile access in Nigeria have grown to the point where tens of millions of people transact online, the great majority of the country's roughly 39 million MSMEs have not moved onto a proper e-commerce platform, held back by cost, limited technical capacity, concerns about trust, and the poor contextual fit of generic international tools. It also identified a parallel gap on the technical side: modern web technologies such as Next.js, TypeScript, and GraphQL are powerful and widely used in industry, yet there is little academic documentation of how they fit together end to end in a complete online store, and even less that ties such work to the realities of the Nigerian market.

Chapter Two reviewed the relevant literature. It clarified the core concepts, set the work within a theoretical framework combining Design Science Research with the Technology Acceptance Model and Diffusion of Innovations theory, examined the Nigerian e-commerce and SME context with concrete statistics, and surveyed both the empirical research on the chosen technologies and the existing systems on the market. The review confirmed a clear gap that the study was positioned to fill.

Chapter Three set out the methodology and design. Following a design science approach realised through an iterative, agile-inspired process, it specified the system's requirements and presented its layered architecture, database design, GraphQL API, interface, and security design, including the resolution of the authentication-context challenge identified earlier.

Chapter Four reported the implementation and results. It documented the real folder structure, database schemas, and GraphQL API, walked through each module with code drawn from the actual codebase, and presented the results of functional testing across twenty-five test cases together with a performance evaluation of the deployed application. The evidence showed a working, deployed system that behaved as required.

5.4 Answering the Research Questions

Beyond the formal objectives, it is worth returning to the four research questions posed in Chapter One, since they framed the whole investigation. Addressing each in turn shows how the study's findings come together into a coherent answer.

Research Question 1 asked how a full-stack e-commerce platform can be designed and enhanced using Next.js, TypeScript, GraphQL, and MongoDB to meet the core needs of SMEs. The answer, demonstrated throughout Chapters Three and Four, lies in a layered, modular architecture that places a single GraphQL endpoint over a Mongoose data layer, uses Next.js server components for the data-rich, search-indexable pages and client components for interactive elements, and keeps business logic, such as pricing and order finalisation, in a dedicated service layer. The core commerce needs, browsing, searching, a cart, a secure checkout, payment, orders, and reviews, were each implemented and tested. The decisive design idea was the separation of a reusable commerce engine from a customisable presentation layer, which is what makes the architecture genuinely suited to many SMEs rather than to one.

Research Question 2 asked what the principal challenges of integrating GraphQL authentication context within the Next.js App Router are, and how they can be resolved. The principal challenge proved to be ensuring that the identity and role of the requesting user are reliably available to every resolver, despite the App Router's separation of server and client components and the stateless nature of the API. The resolution, set out in §4.5, is to verify the token's signature and assemble the user context inside the GraphQL route handler so that it is supplied to all resolvers, while a complementary Edge route gate provides a fast first line of defence before pages are served. This arrangement enforces authorisation at the server boundary, independently of anything the client interface does.

Research Question 3 asked to what extent the enhanced platform satisfies accepted criteria for functionality, performance, usability, and security. The functional testing of §4.17 showed correct behaviour across all twenty-five test cases spanning every module; the performance evaluation of §4.18, using Google Lighthouse, assessed speed and search-engine readiness on representative pages; usability was addressed by designing against established heuristics and accessibility guidance; and security was built in through server-authoritative pricing, verified payments, idempotent order creation, hashed passwords, and server-enforced route protection, in line with OWASP guidance. Together these provide credible evidence that the platform meets the criteria set for it.

Research Question 4 asked how applicable the resulting model is as an affordable, deployable e-commerce solution for Nigerian SMEs. The platform supports the payment channels Nigerians actually use (card, bank transfer, and USSD), prices in naira, and can be operated by a non-technical owner after brief training; its reusable-engine design allows a new store to be produced by changing configuration rather than rewriting code, which keeps the cost of delivery low. On this basis the model is well suited to the Nigerian SME context, subject to the limitations noted in §5.6.

5.5 Conclusion

Two broad conclusions follow from the study. The first is technical. It is both feasible and beneficial to build a complete, production-oriented e-commerce platform on a modern stack of Next.js, TypeScript, GraphQL, and MongoDB. The combination delivers real advantages: server rendering improves speed and discoverability, static typing improves reliability, and GraphQL provides precise, efficient data access. The specific difficulty of propagating authentication context within the Next.js App Router, which the literature had left largely unexamined, can be resolved cleanly by verifying the token and assembling the context in the GraphQL route handler. In demonstrating this end to end, with working code and tests, the study contributes a documented answer to a question that practitioners have until now had to solve in isolation. Equally, the study shows that the much-discussed trade-offs between these technologies are manageable in practice: the small overheads of, for instance, maintaining a typed schema are repaid many times over in reliability and clarity.

The second conclusion is practical and contextual. By separating a reusable commerce engine from a customisable presentation layer, and by supporting locally relevant payment channels, naira pricing, and owner-managed operation, the platform addresses the very barriers that the literature identified as holding Nigerian SMEs back from formal e-commerce. The result is not merely a prototype but a model that could realistically be deployed for a small business at low cost, and re-skinned for many such businesses from a single tested core. In short, the study shows that a modern, well-engineered platform and the needs of a developing-market SME are not in tension; with the right architectural choices, the one can be made to serve the other. This is, ultimately, the contribution the study most hopes to make: not only a piece of working software, but evidence that careful engineering and genuine accessibility for small businesses can be pursued together rather than as competing goals.

5.6 Contributions to Knowledge

This study makes the following contributions:

  1. A fully functional, enhanced, and documented e-commerce web application built on a modern full-stack JavaScript architecture, available as a reference implementation.
  2. A documented resolution of the authentication-context propagation problem within the Next.js App Router GraphQL endpoint, an issue inadequately addressed in the existing literature.
  3. An end-to-end account of the design and implementation of a full-stack e-commerce system using a TypeScript-first methodology, from database schema to user interface, suitable for teaching and for guiding practice.
  4. A reusable architectural model, separating a commerce engine from interchangeable presentation themes, that reduces the time and cost of delivering bespoke stores for multiple clients.
  5. A contextual analysis connecting modern architectural practice to the concrete requirements of SME e-commerce in the Nigerian digital economy.

5.7 Limitations of the Study

The conclusions should be read in light of several limitations, stated honestly. First, the platform was evaluated in a development and staging context; it was not subjected to large-scale, real-world transactional load, so its behaviour under heavy concurrent traffic remains to be confirmed. Second, payment integration was exercised in sandbox mode in accordance with the regulatory requirements for live transactions, meaning that the live-payment configuration is documented but not transacted against. Third, usability evidence rests on heuristic evaluation and a small number of representative users rather than a large-sample study, which limits statistical generalisation. Fourth, the findings are framed within the Nigerian context and may require adaptation before applying elsewhere. None of these limitations undermines the study's central contributions, but each marks a sensible boundary on the strength of its claims.

5.8 Recommendations

5.8.1 Recommendations for practice

  • Small businesses seeking an online presence should consider a reusable, self-owned platform of this kind as a lower-cost alternative to recurring subscription services, particularly where local payment channels and naira pricing matter.
  • Developers building on a Next.js and GraphQL stack should verify authentication tokens and assemble the request context at the GraphQL route handler, and enforce authorisation in resolvers, rather than relying on the client interface to hide privileged actions.
  • For any store handling payment, prices should be recomputed on the server and payment outcomes verified with the provider, with order finalisation made idempotent to prevent duplicate orders.

5.8.2 Recommendations for future work

  • Load and scalability testing: evaluate the platform under realistic concurrent load and tune database indexing and caching accordingly.
  • Live deployment study: deploy with a real SME and measure actual sales, conversion, and user satisfaction over time, moving beyond the staging evaluation conducted here.
  • Expanded payment and logistics options: integrate additional gateways and delivery/logistics providers relevant to the Nigerian market.
  • Analytics and recommendations: add product recommendation and richer analytics to support data-driven merchandising.
  • Theme marketplace: extend the theming system into a library of ready-made themes, further reducing the effort of launching a new client store.
  • Formal usability study: conduct a larger usability evaluation using a validated instrument such as the System Usability Scale to strengthen the evidence base.

5.9 Reflections and Lessons Learned

A research project of this kind teaches lessons that do not always fit neatly under "findings", but which are worth recording because they would guide anyone attempting similar work. Three stand out.

The first concerns the value of doing security work early rather than late. Several of the most important decisions in the project, recomputing prices on the server, verifying payments with the provider, and making order creation idempotent, were not cosmetic features but structural choices that would have been painful to retrofit. Building them in from the start, guided by the OWASP principles reviewed in Chapter Two, proved far easier than it would have been to add them to a finished but insecure system. The lesson is that in any application handling money, security and correctness must be treated as part of the foundation, not as a finishing touch.

The second lesson concerns the payoff of investing in structure. The decision to separate a reusable commerce engine from a customisable presentation layer required extra discipline during development, since it is always quicker, in the short term, to hard-code values where they are needed. Yet this investment is precisely what turns a one-off project into a reusable asset, and it is what allows a new store to be produced through configuration rather than rewriting. Good structure, in other words, is not an academic nicety; it is what converts effort spent once into value realised many times.

The third lesson is about context. It would have been possible to build a technically excellent platform that nonetheless failed in the Nigerian market because it ignored how people there actually pay and shop. Engaging seriously with the local context, the prominence of bank transfer and USSD, the role of messaging apps, and acute cost sensitivity, shaped the design in ways that a purely technical perspective would have missed. The broader lesson is that technology decisions and user-context decisions are not separate activities; the best designs emerge when they inform each other.

5.10 Concluding Remark

This study began from a simple observation: that a great many capable Nigerian businesses remain offline, not for lack of customers or ambition, but for lack of an affordable, trustworthy, and locally suitable way to sell online. By designing, building, documenting, and evaluating FanMidCommerce, the study has shown that modern web technology can be marshalled to meet that need, and has left behind a working system and a reusable model that others can learn from and build upon. The work is offered both as a completed piece of research and as a foundation for the continued, practical digital inclusion of small enterprises.
