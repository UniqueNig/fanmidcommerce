ABSTRACT

The expansion of internet and mobile connectivity in Nigeria has created a substantial digital marketplace, yet many small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) remain excluded from formal e-commerce by the cost of proprietary platforms, limited technical capacity, and the poor contextual fit of generic international solutions. At the same time, although modern web technologies, server-rendered React frameworks, statically typed JavaScript, and application programming interface (API)-first designs such as GraphQL, offer significant advantages in performance, maintainability, and developer productivity, the academic literature contains few integrated, end-to-end accounts of building a production-oriented e-commerce system on such a stack. This study addresses both gaps through the design, enhancement, and documentation of FanMidCommerce, a full-stack e-commerce web application built with Next.js (App Router), TypeScript, GraphQL with Apollo Server, and the MongoDB document database, and supported by the Paystack payment gateway and the Resend transactional-email service. Adopting a design science research methodology combined with an iterative, agile-inspired development process, the study analyses the existing codebase, enhances it with product search and filtering, order-management workflows, payment integration, and customer reviews, and resolves a recognised technical challenge concerning the propagation of authentication context to GraphQL resolvers within the Next.js App Router. The enhanced platform is evaluated for functionality, performance, usability, and security using established instruments, including Nielsen’s usability heuristics, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, and the OWASP security guidance. The study contributes a documented, reusable reference implementation for SME-oriented e-commerce, an analysis of GraphQL–Next.js integration suitable for researchers and practitioners, and an assessment of the platform’s applicability within the Nigerian digital economy. Chapters One to Three, presented here, cover the introduction, the review of related literature, and the research methodology and system design.

Keywords: e-commerce; full-stack web development; Next.js; GraphQL; MongoDB; TypeScript; design science; small and medium-sized enterprises; Nigeria.

Chapter One

Introduction

1.1 Background to the Study

Over the past two decades, the internet has changed the way people buy and sell almost everywhere, and this change has been especially dramatic in developing regions such as sub-Saharan Africa. As mobile networks have spread, smartphones have become cheaper, and the cost of data has fallen, more and more people have come online for the first time. The result is a large and fast-growing digital marketplace (International Telecommunication Union [ITU], 2023; GSMA, 2023). Nigeria sits at the centre of this shift. As the most populous country on the continent and its largest economy, it has seen internet penetration climb to roughly 55.4 per cent of the population by early 2023, while broadband penetration stood at about 44.4 per cent by the end of 2024 (Nigerian Communications Commission [NCC], 2024). In practical terms, tens of millions of Nigerians now carry an internet-connected device, and a growing number of them are comfortable browsing, comparing, and ordering goods online. This is the foundation on which a maturing electronic-commerce sector is being built.

What exactly is electronic commerce? At its simplest, e-commerce is the buying and selling of goods and services, together with the related movement of money and information, carried out over electronic networks such as the internet (Laudon & Traver, 2021). That definition, however, hides how much work a real platform has to do. A working online store is not just a page that displays products. It has to manage a product catalogue, handle customer accounts, run a shopping cart and a checkout, process payments, track orders through to delivery, and keep customers informed afterwards. All of these parts must fit together smoothly. When they do, customers are more likely to complete a purchase, return for another, and trust the business with their money; when they do not, sales are lost at every step (Laudon & Traver, 2021). For a business of any size, getting this right means it can reach customers far beyond its physical location and sell around the clock, without paying for extra shop space.

In Nigeria, the businesses that stand to gain the most from this are the micro, small and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs), and there are a great many of them. The most recent national survey, conducted jointly by the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria (SMEDAN) and the National Bureau of Statistics, counted roughly 39 million MSMEs. Together they account for about 46.3 per cent of the country's gross domestic product, some 87.9 per cent of national employment, and around 96.9 per cent of all businesses (National Bureau of Statistics & SMEDAN, 2021). These figures make one point very clearly: small businesses are not a side note in the Nigerian economy; they are its backbone. For them, selling online is a chance to reach new customers and to become more resilient when times are hard.

Yet, despite the opportunity, comparatively few of these businesses have moved onto a proper e-commerce platform. Several obstacles stand in the way. The first is cost: many established platforms charge a recurring subscription that is difficult for a small trader to justify month after month. The second is skill: building and maintaining a website calls for technical knowledge that most business owners simply do not have. The third is trust, both the customer's worry about paying online and the owner's worry about fraud and chargebacks. There are also practical problems with delivery and returns (World Bank, 2019). On top of all this sits a barrier that is easy to overlook but matters a great deal in practice, namely contextual fit. International platforms are often built for other markets. They may not price comfortably in naira, may not support the bank-transfer and Unstructured Supplementary Service Data (USSD) payment channels that many Nigerians prefer, and rarely fit the very common habit of arranging orders through messaging apps such as WhatsApp. Faced with these hurdles, a large number of capable businesses settle for an informal online presence, usually a social-media page paired with manual order-taking, which lacks the structure, security, and room to grow that a dedicated platform would provide.

While the market has been changing, so too has the technology used to build websites, and it has changed quickly. Modern web development increasingly favours full-stack JavaScript, and in particular TypeScript, where a single language and a single type system run across both the part of the application the user sees (the front end) and the part that runs on the server (the back end). It favours server-rendered React frameworks such as Next.js, which manage to combine the smooth interactivity people expect from a modern app with the speed and search-engine visibility that come from rendering pages on the server. And it increasingly favours newer ways of moving data between client and server, such as GraphQL, which lets the application ask for exactly the data it needs and nothing more (Wieruch, 2022; Vercel, 2024). Taken together, these tools promise faster, more reliable, and more maintainable software than the older technology on which many existing online stores are built. The difficulty is that, although industry has adopted them eagerly, there is still very little careful, academic documentation showing how they work together from start to finish in a complete, real-world online store, and even less that ties such technical work to the day-to-day realities of a market such as Nigeria's.

This study sits precisely where those two gaps meet: an under-served market of small businesses on one side, and a powerful but under-documented set of modern tools on the other. Its subject is FanMidCommerce, a full-stack e-commerce web application that the researcher has built using Next.js (with the App Router), TypeScript, GraphQL served through Apollo Server, the MongoDB document database accessed through the Mongoose library, and the Tailwind CSS styling framework, with payment and email handled by the Paystack and Resend services. The application already provides a customer-facing storefront, a customer account dashboard, and an administrative panel. The work reported here extends that foundation with further commerce features, resolves a number of technical problems encountered along the way (most notably how authentication information is carried through to GraphQL resolvers inside the App Router), and documents the design decisions in detail. The intention is to deliver two things at once: an academic contribution, in the form of a carefully documented case study and a reusable reference implementation, and a practical contribution, in the form of a low-cost, locally suited e-commerce platform that Nigerian small businesses could actually adopt.

1.2 Statement of the Problem

E-commerce has grown rapidly worldwide, but growth in the marketplace has not been matched by clear, well-documented guidance on how to build the platforms that power it, at least not for the modern JavaScript frameworks and API-first designs that now dominate professional practice. A developer or a student who wants to build a complete online store on these newer tools will find plenty of short tutorials covering one feature at a time, but very little that shows the whole system fitting together, and almost nothing written with academic rigour. This study is motivated by five related problems that together describe this gap.

First, a reliance on legacy architectures. A large share of the e-commerce solutions in use today are built on older technology stacks. These work, but they do not take advantage of the rendering speed, the type safety, or the easier maintenance that modern frameworks such as Next.js, with its App Router, now offer. As a consequence, anyone wishing to adopt the newer approach has few complete, end-to-end examples to learn from. The academic literature, where it touches these tools at all, tends to look at one technique in isolation rather than showing how the pieces combine into a working whole.

Second, the difficulty of integrating GraphQL within the Next.js App Router. Running a GraphQL API from inside a Next.js App Router route handler turns out to be harder than it first appears. The thorniest part is reliably building and passing along what GraphQL calls the context: the small bundle of per-request information, such as who the user is and what role they hold, that every resolver needs in order to decide whether the request should be allowed. If this context is not assembled correctly and made available everywhere it is needed, access control breaks down, with obvious consequences for security. Despite how central this is, the published literature barely addresses it, so developers are left to work out a solution on their own, often by trial and error. The present study treats this challenge as a first-class research concern and documents a working resolution.

Third, a shortage of end-to-end development documentation. Very little published work follows a full-stack e-commerce application through its whole life cycle, from the design of the database schema, through the specification of the API, to the building of the user interface, all using a TypeScript-first approach. Accounts of this kind are genuinely useful: they help students learn, they guide practitioners, and they make good patterns reproducible. Their scarcity in the peer-reviewed literature is itself part of the problem this study sets out to address.

Fourth, the habit of treating usability and accessibility as afterthoughts. In many academic prototypes, the way a system looks and feels, and whether people with different abilities can actually use it, is bolted on near the end rather than designed in from the start. This is a mistake. It weakens the prototype as something that could really be deployed, and it ignores a well-established body of evidence showing that usability and trust strongly influence whether customers adopt an online store at all. This study therefore treats usability and accessibility as requirements in their own right.

Fifth, a mismatch with the local context. Even when capable platforms do exist, they often do not fit the day-to-day reality of running a small business in Nigeria. They may be too expensive, may not support the payment channels customers actually use, and may assume a level of technical staffing that a small owner-run business does not have. Pulling these five problems together, there is a clear need for an e-commerce platform that is built on modern architecture, is documented thoroughly enough to be learned from and reused, resolves the integration challenges noted above, treats usability and accessibility as central, and is deliberately shaped to fit the small-business context. FanMidCommerce is the vehicle through which this study sets out to meet that need.

1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study

1.3.1 Aim

The aim of this study is to enhance and comprehensively document FanMidCommerce, a full-stack e-commerce web application, in order to demonstrate the practical application of modern web-development technologies and architectural patterns within an academic research framework, and to assess its suitability as an e-commerce solution for SMEs in the Nigerian context.

1.3.2 Objectives

The specific objectives of the study are to:

  1. review existing literature on e-commerce platform architectures, full-stack web-development frameworks, and API-driven application design;
  2. conduct a comprehensive analysis of the existing FanMidCommerce codebase in order to identify areas for functional and architectural enhancement;
  3. design and implement additional features, including product search and filtering, order-management workflows, payment-gateway integration, and customer review functionality;
  4. resolve identified technical challenges relating to GraphQL context management and authentication within the Next.js App Router environment;
  5. evaluate the performance, usability, and security of the enhanced application using appropriate assessment frameworks; and
  6. produce thorough technical documentation covering the system architecture, database schema, API design, and deployment procedures, and assess the applicability of the platform model for Nigerian SMEs.

1.4 Research Questions

Consistent with the objectives, the study is guided by the following research questions:

  1. How can a full-stack e-commerce platform be designed and enhanced using Next.js, TypeScript, GraphQL, and MongoDB to satisfy the core commerce requirements of SMEs?
  2. What are the principal technical challenges of integrating GraphQL authentication context within the Next.js App Router, and how may they be resolved?
  3. To what extent does the enhanced FanMidCommerce platform satisfy accepted criteria for functionality, performance, usability, and security?
  4. How applicable is the resulting platform model as an affordable and deployable e-commerce solution for SMEs within the Nigerian digital economy?

1.5 Significance of the Study

This study matters to several different groups of people, and it is worth being specific about how.

For the academic community, it offers something that is currently in short supply: a documented, real-world case study of building a full-stack e-commerce application on a modern TypeScript-first stack. Much of the existing academic writing on web development examines one idea at a time, a rendering strategy here, an API pattern there. By following a single, complete system from database to interface, this work shows how the parts actually interact. Its detailed treatment of how authentication context is carried across the Next.js App Router GraphQL boundary speaks to a specific and recognised gap, and the structured way the system is evaluated can serve as a template for assessing similar projects in future.

For practitioners and the wider open-source community, the enhanced platform is more than an academic exercise; it is something that could actually be deployed for a real small business at low cost. A particular design decision makes this possible. The architecture deliberately separates a reusable "commerce engine", the parts that handle products, carts, checkout, orders, payments, and administration, from a customisable presentation layer that controls how each store looks. Because of this split, one well-tested core can be re-skinned for many different businesses without rewriting the underlying logic. In practice, this turns what is usually a costly, build-from-scratch project into a far quicker configuration exercise, which is exactly the kind of saving a small business needs. The documentation that accompanies the code lowers the barrier further, making it realistic for another developer or a student to pick the system up, deploy it, and extend it.

For the Nigerian small-business sector, the study contributes to a larger goal: widening access to digital-commerce tools that actually fit local conditions, pricing in naira, supporting the payment channels people really use, and being simple enough for an owner to run after a short period of training. In a small way, this supports the digital inclusion of enterprises that the formal e-commerce sector has so far left behind. Finally, for students and educators, the work doubles as a teaching resource: a reference implementation that shows, in one coherent place, how today's web technologies combine into a production-ready whole, filling the space between scattered online tutorials and formal academic texts.

1.6 Scope and Limitations of the Study

1.6.1 Scope

The study encompasses the enhancement and documentation of FanMidCommerce in its entirety, including the customer storefront, the customer account dashboard, and the administrative panel. The technology scope is bounded by the existing stack: Next.js (App Router) with TypeScript, GraphQL served through Apollo Server, MongoDB accessed via the Mongoose library, and Tailwind CSS for styling, together with the Paystack payment gateway, the Resend transactional-email service, and the Cloudinary media service. The research comprises a literature review, an analysis of the existing system, iterative design and implementation, and testing and evaluation. Functionally, it covers catalogue browsing with search, filtering, and pagination; product variants and stock management; customer authentication and account management; cart and secure checkout; payment integration; order management and notification; customer reviews; and administrative management of the foregoing.

1.6.2 Limitations

Several limitations are acknowledged. First, the platform is deployed to a development or staging environment for evaluation; large-scale, real-world transactional load testing lies beyond the present scope, so claims regarding behaviour under heavy concurrent load are necessarily provisional. Second, payment-gateway integration is exercised in sandbox/test mode, in keeping with the regulatory requirements governing live financial transactions; the live-payment configuration is documented but not transacted against. Third, usability evaluation is conducted with a limited number of representative participants rather than a large sample, which constrains statistical generalisation. Fourth, the findings are contextualised within the Nigerian digital landscape and may require adaptation before they generalise to other settings. These limitations qualify the strength of certain claims but do not diminish the study’s central contributions of design, implementation, and documentation.

2.2 Conceptual Review

2.2.1 Electronic Commerce and Its Models

Electronic commerce covers the whole range of commercial activity that takes place over digital networks: selling goods online, taking electronic payments, and the flow of information that passes between buyers, sellers, and the middlemen who connect them (Laudon & Traver, 2021). It is usually grouped according to who is trading with whom. In business-to-consumer (B2C) commerce, a business sells directly to individual shoppers; in business-to-business (B2B), one business sells to another; in consumer-to-consumer (C2C), individuals sell to one another, normally through a marketplace that brings them together; and in consumer-to-business (C2B), individuals offer goods or services to businesses. FanMidCommerce belongs firmly in the first category. It is a B2C platform, built so that a single business can sell straight to its own customers, which is the most common need among the small enterprises this study is concerned with.

Describing a B2C system as "a website that sells things" understates how many moving parts it really has. Underneath, it must hold a product catalogue, manage customer accounts, run a shopping cart and a checkout, take and verify payment, move each order through the stages of fulfilment, and keep the customer informed at every point. None of these parts is impressive on its own; what matters is how smoothly they work together. A checkout that stalls, a payment that is hard to trust, or an order whose status is never communicated will each cost the business a sale. The research literature is consistent on this point: the smoothness of the experience, and the trust and perceived ease of use it creates, has a direct bearing on how many visitors become buyers, how many return, and therefore on whether the business succeeds online at all (Laudon & Traver, 2021). This is why the chapters that follow treat the quality of the experience as seriously as the correctness of the code.

2.2.2 Full-Stack Web Development and TypeScript

The phrase "full-stack" simply means working on the whole application rather than just one part of it: the interface the user sees, the logic that runs on the server, and the database that stores everything in between. What makes the modern JavaScript ecosystem unusual is that all three layers can be written in the same language. A developer no longer has to switch mentally between, say, JavaScript in the browser and a different language on the server, and the same code and data structures can often be shared across the boundary (Mikowski & Powell, 2013). TypeScript, a statically typed superset of JavaScript, strengthens this model by introducing a type system that surfaces many classes of error at compile time rather than at run time, thereby improving reliability and maintainability, qualities of particular importance in a transactional system where defects can have financial consequences. The benefit is not merely theoretical: in a large-scale empirical study, Gao, Bird, and Barr (2017) added type annotations to code containing known, publicly reported JavaScript defects and found that a static type checker would have detected approximately 15 per cent of those bugs before release, providing concrete evidence that static typing prevents a meaningful share of real-world faults. The enduring value of clear module boundaries and consistent data contracts across the client–server boundary (Mikowski & Powell, 2013) further informs the layered, modular architecture adopted in this study.

2.2.3 The Next.js Framework and Rendering Strategies

Next.js is a framework built on top of the React library. Where React on its own leaves many decisions to the developer, Next.js supplies the surrounding machinery: it can render pages on the server (server-side rendering, or SSR), build them ahead of time as static files (static site generation, SSG), refresh those static pages periodically (incremental static regeneration, ISR), route requests based on the folder structure of the project, and bundle everything for production through an integrated build pipeline (Vercel, 2024). Its newer App Router introduces a distinction that matters a great deal for this study. Some components are server components, which run on the server and can reach back-end resources such as the database directly; others are client components, which run in the browser and provide the interactivity users expect. The availability of multiple rendering strategies is significant for e-commerce: server rendering and static generation improve initial load performance and search-engine visibility, both of which affect discoverability and conversion, while client components support the interactive elements of cart and checkout. This separation, however, requires careful reasoning about where data fetching and authentication take place, a theme that recurs throughout the study. Search-engine optimisation, in particular, benefits from the server-rendered, crawlable markup that the framework produces for catalogue and product pages.

2.2.4 API Architectures: REST and GraphQL

An application programming interface, or API, is the agreed channel through which the front end and the back end exchange data. For many years the dominant style has been Representational State Transfer (REST), described by Fielding (2000), which organises an API around "resources" reached through a uniform set of operations, each address returning a fixed bundle of data. REST is mature, well understood, and works well, but it has two recurring inefficiencies. Sometimes an endpoint returns more than the screen actually needs, wasting bandwidth (over-fetching); at other times a single screen needs data from several endpoints, forcing the client to make multiple round trips (under-fetching). GraphQL, by contrast, is a query language and runtime in which a typed schema defines the data a client may request, and the client specifies precisely the fields it requires (GraphQL Foundation, 2021; Wieruch, 2022). This client-driven model mitigates over- and under-fetching and provides a strongly typed contract that aligns naturally with a TypeScript codebase. Apollo Server is a widely used GraphQL implementation that supports schema definition, resolver composition, and a per-request context object through which shared information, notably the authenticated user, is supplied to resolvers (Apollo GraphQL, 2024). The reliable construction of this context within a Next.js App Router route handler constitutes a specific technical concern of the present study, and is treated in detail in Chapter Three.

2.2.5 Data Storage: Relational and Document Databases

Persistent storage may be provided by relational (SQL) databases, which organise data into fixed-schema tables related by keys, or by non-relational (NoSQL) databases, of which the document model is one variety. MongoDB is a document-oriented database that stores data as flexible, JSON-like documents rather than as rows in rigid tables (MongoDB, Inc., 2024). This model is well suited to the varied and evolving data structures common in e-commerce, for example, products that may or may not carry sizes, colours, or multiple images, and supports rapid iteration during development. The flexibility of the document model nonetheless places greater responsibility on the developer for disciplined data modelling, including deliberate decisions about embedding related data within a parent document versus referencing it across documents, and for appropriate indexing to preserve query performance as data grows. In this study, MongoDB is accessed through Mongoose, which layers schemas, validation, and a typed interface over the underlying database.

2.2.6 Authentication, Authorisation, and Security

Two related ideas are easy to confuse but important to separate. Authentication answers the question "who are you?", confirming a user's identity; authorisation answers the next question, "what are you allowed to do?", deciding which actions that identity may perform. A common way to handle the first is the JSON Web Token (JWT), a small, digitally signed token that the server can check on every request without having to remember sessions on its own side, an approach described as "stateless". In an application that separates customer and administrative areas, authorisation must be enforced consistently at the server boundary, so that privileged operations cannot be invoked by unauthorised clients irrespective of the state of the user interface. The Open Web Application Security Project (OWASP, 2021) identifies broken access control and cryptographic failures among the foremost categories of web-application risk; its guidance, together with the practice of recomputing sensitive values such as prices on the server and verifying payment outcomes with the provider, informs the security design presented in Chapter Three.

2.2.7 Online Payment Integration

Payment is the point at which an e-commerce transaction is consummated, and its integrity and convenience are decisive for consumer trust. Payment gateways abstract the complexity and regulatory burden of handling card and bank data by tokenising sensitive details and processing transactions on the merchant’s behalf. In the Nigerian context, locally established gateways such as Paystack support not only card payments but also bank transfer and USSD, channels that are widely used and, for many consumers, more trusted than card entry. Secure integration requires that the merchant application initiate transactions against the gateway, redirect or collect payment through the gateway’s secure interface, and independently verify the outcome with the gateway before fulfilling an order, rather than trusting any value reported by the client. These practices, together with idempotent order finalisation to prevent duplicate processing, are central to the design described later.

2.2.8 Theming, Usability, and Accessibility

The presentation layer determines, in large measure, whether a platform is adopted and trusted. Tailwind CSS is a utility-first styling framework that promotes consistent, responsive interfaces, while libraries such as next-themes enable dynamic light/dark theming through CSS custom properties. Beyond aesthetics, established usability heuristics (Nielsen, 1994), including visibility of system status, error prevention and recovery, consistency, and a match between the system and the real world, provide criteria against which an interface can be both designed and evaluated. Accessibility, codified in the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (W3C, 2018), adds requirements such as sufficient colour contrast, keyboard operability, and semantic structure, ensuring usability by people with diverse abilities. Treating these criteria as first-class requirements, embedded throughout development rather than appended at the end, is a stated commitment of this study and a direct response to a gap identified in Section 1.2.

2.3 Theoretical Framework

A study of this kind needs more than one theoretical lens, because it is doing more than one thing. It is building something, so it needs a theory of how knowledge is created through building; and it hopes the result will be adopted, so it needs theories that explain why people take up a new technology. The framework adopted here therefore brings together one methodological theory, Design Science Research, with two well-established adoption theories, the Technology Acceptance Model and Diffusion of Innovations, and rests all three on a foundation of long-standing software-engineering principles. Each is introduced in turn below, with a note on how it shapes the present work.

2.3.1 Design Science Research

The main methodological lens is Design Science Research (DSR). Its central claim is that genuine knowledge can come not only from observing the world but from carefully building and then rigorously evaluating an artefact that solves a real problem (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004). Hevner et al. (2004) propose guidelines requiring that the research produce a viable artefact, that the artefact address a relevant problem, that it be evaluated rigorously, and that the work make a clear contribution and be communicated effectively. Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, and Chatterjee (2007) operationalise this through a Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) comprising problem identification, definition of objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication. This study maps directly onto these stages: the problem and objectives are established in Chapter One, the design and development are detailed in Chapter Three and realised in Chapter Four, and demonstration, evaluation, and communication follow.

2.3.2 The Technology Acceptance Model

To frame the platform’s intended adoption by SME operators and their customers, the study draws on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which posits that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are the primary determinants of a user’s intention to adopt a technology (Davis, 1989). TAM provides a rationale for the study’s emphasis on usability and accessibility: a platform that is perceived as difficult to use is unlikely to be adopted, however technically capable it may be. The model thus links the interface-design objectives to the broader goal of practical applicability.

2.3.3 Diffusion of Innovations

Complementing TAM, Rogers’ (2003) Diffusion of Innovations theory explains how innovations spread through a social system and identifies attributes that influence the rate of adoption, including relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability. These attributes are pertinent to the design choices made in this study: the separation of a reusable engine from a customisable presentation layer improves compatibility and reduces the effective complexity of deploying new stores, while low cost and local contextual fit enhance relative advantage for SMEs.

2.3.4 Software-Engineering Principles

Finally, the way the artefact is actually built is guided by software-engineering principles that have stood the test of time: modularity, separation of concerns, abstraction, and information hiding, all of which help keep a system maintainable and easy to extend (Sommerville, 2016; Pressman & Maxim, 2020). These principles are expressed concretely in the layered architecture and in the deliberate division between commerce logic and presentation described in Chapter Three.

2.4 E-Commerce and SMEs in the Nigerian Context

No technology choice makes sense in a vacuum, so it is worth looking closely at the market this platform is meant to serve. Nigeria is large, young, and increasingly connected, yet the take-up of formal e-commerce among its small businesses is still patchy (ITU, 2023; GSMA, 2023; World Bank, 2019). The raw numbers show why the opportunity is so striking. Internet penetration had reached roughly 55.4 per cent of the population by early 2023, and broadband penetration about 44.4 per cent by the close of 2024 (NCC, 2024), while the national MSME survey counted some 39 million micro, small and medium-sized enterprises contributing around 46.3 per cent of gross domestic product and 87.9 per cent of employment (National Bureau of Statistics & SMEDAN, 2021). Put side by side, these figures reveal a striking gap: the connectivity is there and the businesses are there, yet relatively few of those businesses have moved their trade onto a proper online platform. Closing that gap is precisely the practical problem this study speaks to, and several features of the local context shape what a suitable platform must do. First, connectivity is predominantly mobile: most users access the internet through smartphones over mobile networks, which makes a mobile-first, performance-conscious interface essential rather than optional. Second, payment behaviour is heterogeneous: alongside card payments, bank transfers and USSD-based transactions are widely used and, for many consumers, more familiar and more trusted; a platform that supports only card entry therefore excludes a meaningful segment of potential buyers. Third, trust and informality coexist: a significant volume of trade is coordinated through social media and messaging applications, and consumers often prefer to confirm details through such channels before or instead of a formal checkout. Fourth, cost sensitivity is acute: recurring platform fees weigh heavily on small traders, favouring solutions with low or one-off costs.

The barriers to adoption reported in the literature align closely with these factors. In a study of SME operators across Nigeria’s six geo-political zones, Awa, Awara, and Lebari (2015) identified critical inhibitors including weak firm finances, firm size, non-transparency in government support, location factors, and concerns over the confidentiality of information. Agwu and Murray (2015), in an empirical investigation of small and medium-scale businesses, similarly found that the absence of a robust regulatory and security framework for e-commerce, limited technical skills, and inadequate basic infrastructure were principal barriers to adoption. These findings echo broader assessments that cite the cost of proprietary platforms, limited technical capacity, concerns over payment security and trust, and logistics constraints relating to delivery and returns (World Bank, 2019). A platform designed for this market must therefore accommodate naira-denominated pricing; support card, transfer, and USSD payment channels; bridge formal checkout and messaging-based enquiry; and be operable by a non-technical owner after minimal training. These contextual requirements are carried forward directly into the requirements analysis of Chapter Three.

2.5 Empirical Review of Related Studies

The concepts and context set out so far explain what the study is doing and why. This section turns to a different kind of evidence: studies that have actually tested, under controlled or comparative conditions, the very technologies and adoption questions on which this work depends. Reviewing them serves two purposes. It grounds the design choices made later in measured findings rather than mere preference, and it surfaces the limitations and disagreements in the evidence, which a careful design has to take into account. The discussion is organised into eight strands, moving from the technical (how REST and GraphQL compare; how Next.js affects performance and search visibility; how document and relational databases behave; what static typing does for software quality; and what is known about web-application security) through to the human and contextual (what drives e-commerce adoption in developing economies and Nigeria; how usability and trust relate to purchase intention; and what shapes digital-payment adoption in Nigeria). For each, the aim is to say plainly what was done, what was found, and what the finding means for the present study.

2.5.1 Empirical Studies on REST and GraphQL

The choice of GraphQL over REST in this study is supported by a maturing experimental literature. In a frequently cited controlled experiment, Brito and Valente (2020) tasked twenty-two participants with implementing eight queries using both REST and GraphQL and found that GraphQL significantly reduced the effort required to construct remote-service queries, with lower median completion times than REST. In earlier work, Brito, Mombach, and Valente (2019) conducted a practical assessment of migrating REST APIs to GraphQL and reported that GraphQL could substantially reduce the size of JSON responses returned to clients by eliminating over-fetching, although the migration itself demanded careful schema design. Complementary measurement studies have examined run-time behaviour: Seabra, Nazário, and Pinto (2019) found that migrating from REST to GraphQL reduced latency in two of three tested applications, chiefly by reducing the number of network round-trips, although REST outperformed GraphQL under very high request volumes (above roughly three thousand requests). This indicates that GraphQL’s data-transfer benefits are most pronounced for complex, nested queries and moderate loads. The evidence is not uniformly in GraphQL’s favour, however; several performance evaluations note that REST can retain advantages in raw response time and memory efficiency under certain high-load, simple-query conditions. The balance of findings indicates that GraphQL’s principal benefits, precise data selection, fewer round trips, and a strongly typed contract, are most valuable for data-rich, client-diverse applications such as e-commerce storefronts, which is the rationale for its adoption here.

2.5.2 Empirical Studies on Next.js and Server-Side Rendering

The performance and discoverability advantages claimed for server-rendered React frameworks are likewise supported empirically. Pati and Zaki (2025) conducted a comparative analysis of Next.js and a conventional client-rendered React application, measuring metrics including First Contentful Paint and Time to Interactive across varied network and device conditions; they reported that Next.js delivered better overall performance without degrading interaction quality, an effect attributable to server rendering and automated optimisation. Studies focused on search-engine optimisation report a similar pattern: because server rendering delivers fully formed HTML that crawlers can index directly, Next.js applications tend to achieve markedly higher automated SEO scores than equivalent client-rendered React applications (Pati & Zaki, 2025; industry SEO analyses). For an e-commerce platform, where product and category pages must be both fast and discoverable, this body of evidence directly motivates the use of Next.js server components for catalogue pages, as adopted in the present design.

2.5.3 Empirical Studies on Document and Relational Databases

The selection of MongoDB as the data store is informed by comparative database research. In a widely cited web-application study, Gyorodi, Gyorodi, Pecherle, and Olah (2015) benchmarked MongoDB against MySQL across the core create, read, update, and delete operations and found that MongoDB achieved faster performance on several operations, especially bulk insertion of large datasets, attributing the advantage to the document model and the absence of rigid joins. Broader systematic comparisons temper this picture: relational systems retain advantages for complex, multi-table queries and for workloads demanding strict transactional (ACID) consistency, whereas document databases favour flexible schemas and horizontal scalability (Győrödi et al., 2015; systematic reviews of database performance). For an e-commerce catalogue characterised by heterogeneous, evolving product structures, such as items that may or may not carry sizes, colours, or multiple images, the flexibility of the document model is advantageous, provided that disciplined data modelling and indexing are applied, as discussed in Chapter Three.

2.5.4 Empirical Studies on E-Commerce Adoption in Developing Economies and Nigeria

A substantial empirical literature applies adoption theory, particularly the Technology Acceptance Model and its extensions, to online shopping in developing economies, consistently finding that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use shape adoption intentions, alongside context-specific factors such as trust, social influence, and facilitating conditions. Within the Nigerian context specifically, Usman and Kumar (2021) examined the factors influencing consumers’ intention to shop online and emphasised the decisive roles of attitude, trust, and perceived usefulness and ease of use in enhancing the acceptability of online shopping. On the enterprise side, Awa, Awara, and Lebari (2015) identified weak finances, firm size, location, and confidentiality concerns as critical inhibitors of e-commerce adoption among Nigerian SMEs, while Agwu and Murray (2015) highlighted the absence of robust regulatory and security frameworks, limited technical skills, and inadequate infrastructure. A recurring and instructive finding is that Nigerian consumers frequently perceive e-commerce as useful and easy to use yet fail to complete transactions, owing to weak facilitating conditions, including trust, payment, and logistics. This evidence directly motivates several design priorities of the present study: support for locally trusted payment channels, server-side payment verification and security to build trust, and a usable, accessible interface intended to convert favourable perceptions into completed purchases.

2.5.5 Empirical Studies on Static Typing and Software Quality

Because reliability is a stated non-functional requirement of this study, the empirical evidence on static typing merits separate attention. The landmark study by Gao, Bird, and Barr (2017) examined real, publicly reported defects in JavaScript projects and demonstrated that a static type system would have caught roughly 15 per cent of them prior to release, a substantial proportion for a single, low-cost intervention. Subsequent work has produced a more nuanced picture. Comparative analyses of open-source repositories report that TypeScript projects exhibit better code quality and readability than comparable JavaScript projects, although the effect on raw bug-proneness is smaller than often assumed: in a mining study of 604 GitHub projects, Bogner and Merkel (2022) found that TypeScript applications had significantly better code quality and understandability, yet using TypeScript did not automatically yield fewer or more easily fixed bugs. More recent ecosystem studies observe a "shifted fault boundary": as static typing suppresses traditional type and runtime errors, a larger share of remaining faults migrates to build tooling, configuration, and asynchronous error handling. For the present study, the implication is clear: TypeScript is adopted for its demonstrated quality and maintainability benefits, while testing and review remain necessary to address the classes of fault that typing alone does not eliminate.

2.5.6 Empirical Studies on Web Application Security and Authentication

The security design of this study is grounded in a substantial empirical and practitioner literature. The OWASP Top 10 (2021), itself derived from analysis of large volumes of real-world vulnerability data, ranks broken access control and cryptographic failures among the most prevalent and damaging categories of web-application risk. Within the specific area of token-based authentication, security analyses repeatedly identify a recurring and serious class of vulnerability: applications that merely decode a JSON Web Token rather than cryptographically verifying its signature, which can permit user impersonation and privilege escalation. This finding is directly pertinent to the architecture proposed here, in which a fast, decode-only check may be used at the network edge for routing convenience but authoritative signature verification is always performed on the server before any privileged action. Comparative evaluations of security-testing tooling further indicate that no single automated tool detects all vulnerability classes, supporting this study’s use of a structured, OWASP-informed review rather than reliance on a single scanner.

2.5.7 Empirical Studies on Usability, Trust, and Purchase Intention

A consistent empirical literature links website usability and trust to the intention to purchase, which is the ultimate measure of an e-commerce platform’s effectiveness. Studies measuring the influence of usability attributes on purchase intention find that trustworthiness ranks among the most important factors, alongside ease of comprehension and shopping support, and that usability can itself be increased by enhancing trust through clear information, responsive customer care, and visual clarity. Validated instruments, including purpose-built online-shop usability scales and the widely used System Usability Scale, have been employed to quantify these effects, with regression analyses indicating that usability exerts the greatest single influence on buying intention. This evidence substantiates the study’s treatment of usability and accessibility as first-class requirements and informs the choice of evaluation instruments described in Chapter Three.

2.5.8 Empirical Studies on Digital Payment Adoption in Nigeria

Because payment is the point at which an e-commerce transaction succeeds or fails, the empirical literature on digital-payment adoption in Nigeria is especially relevant. Studies applying the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) to mobile money and mobile payment in Nigeria find that performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions shape adoption intentions, and that trust and network effects are particularly influential in the Nigerian context. Multi-country evidence from sub-Saharan Africa further identifies education, bank-account ownership, and mobile-phone access as determinants of adoption. Taken together with the consumer-behaviour finding that Nigerian shoppers often perceive e-commerce favourably yet abandon transactions because of weak facilitating conditions, this literature reinforces a central design decision of the study: to integrate a locally trusted payment gateway supporting card, bank-transfer, and USSD channels, and to verify payment outcomes on the server so as to build the trust that the evidence shows is decisive for transaction completion.

2.6 Review of Related Systems

It also helps to look at the systems a small business could realistically use today, and to be honest about where each falls short for the audience this study has in mind. Broadly, the options fall into four groups: hosted platforms, self-hosted open-source systems, modern headless or API-first frameworks, and fully bespoke builds. Hosted platforms such as Shopify are quick to set up and come with managed infrastructure, but they charge a recurring subscription and give the owner limited control over the underlying code and data, constraints that are significant for cost-sensitive SMEs. Self-hosted open-source systems such as WooCommerce (built on WordPress and PHP) and Magento offer greater control and extensibility but are rooted in older technology stacks and can impose considerable maintenance, performance, and hosting overheads. Modern headless and API-first frameworks such as Medusa and Saleor decouple the storefront from the commerce engine and embrace contemporary tooling, improving flexibility, but they introduce integration complexity and a steeper learning curve that may exceed the capacity of a small business or a solo developer. Bespoke builds afford complete control and contextual fit but, without a reusable foundation, must be re-created for each client at considerable cost.

Within the scholarly and practitioner literature, accounts of building commerce systems on modern React/Next.js and GraphQL foundations tend to address isolated concerns, a particular rendering strategy, a single API pattern, or a discrete feature, rather than documenting an integrated, end-to-end system that also attends to authentication, security, theming, accessibility, and contextual fit. Studies of SME e-commerce adoption in Nigeria, conversely, emphasise economic, infrastructural, and regulatory barriers (Awa et al., 2015; Agwu & Murray, 2015) but seldom connect those findings to concrete architectural choices in a deployable platform. 2.7 Summary of Literature and Research Gap

Drawing the threads of this chapter together, a few things are now clear. The literature confirms that e-commerce holds real strategic value for small businesses, that modern full-stack and API-first architectures bring measurable technical advantages, and that usability, accessibility, and security are not optional extras but central to whether customers adopt and trust an online store. It also paints a clear picture of the Nigerian market in particular: access is mostly mobile, payment behaviour is varied and leans on transfer and USSD as much as cards, formal and informal selling sit side by side, and small businesses are highly sensitive to cost.

Set against this backdrop, three gaps stand out. The first is a shortage of integrated, end-to-end, and properly documented accounts of building a production-ready e-commerce application on a Next.js, TypeScript, GraphQL, and MongoDB stack; the evidence exists in fragments, but rarely as a coherent whole. The second is more specific and more technical: the particular challenge of carrying authentication context through to GraphQL resolvers within the Next.js App Router has been left largely unexamined, even though it is fundamental to security. The third is contextual: very little work connects this kind of modern architectural practice to the concrete, on-the-ground requirements of small-business e-commerce in a market such as Nigeria's. It is these three gaps, taken together, that the present study sets out to close, through the design, enhancement, documentation, and evaluation of FanMidCommerce described in the chapters that follow.

Chapter Three

Research Methodology and System Design

3.1 Introduction

Having set out the problem and reviewed the relevant literature, this chapter explains how the study was actually carried out and how the enhanced FanMidCommerce platform was designed. It moves from the general to the specific: first the overall research design and the development methodology, then the requirements that the system had to meet, and finally the concrete design of the architecture, the database, the API, the interface, and the security, before closing with the tools used and the plan for testing and evaluation. Throughout, it is worth keeping one thing in mind. Unlike a study whose main output is a set of survey results, the main output here is a working, documented piece of software, judged against clearly stated functional and non-functional criteria. That is exactly what the design science research paradigm, introduced in Chapter Two, calls for.

3.2 Research Design

The study follows a design science research (DSR) design, the central idea of which is that new knowledge is produced by building and then carefully evaluating a purposeful artefact, in this case the enhanced FanMidCommerce platform (Hevner et al., 2004; Peffers et al., 2007). This is complemented by a descriptive case-study element, since a single platform is examined in depth to surface design decisions, challenges, and their resolutions. The design is mixed-method: qualitative evidence (architectural analysis, documented design rationale, and usability observation) is combined with quantitative evidence (performance measurements and test outcomes). This orientation is appropriate because the research questions concern how a system can be designed and built, and how well it performs, rather than the testing of hypotheses about a population. The six activities of the Design Science Research Methodology, problem identification, objective definition, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication (Peffers et al., 2007), provide the overarching structure within which the development methodology operates.

3.3 Software Development Methodology

DSR says what kind of research this is, but not how the building itself should be organised. For that, the study uses an iterative, agile-inspired development methodology (Beck et al., 2001; Sommerville, 2016). Instead of attempting the whole enhancement in one long, linear push, the work proceeds in short cycles. Each cycle delivers a small, working improvement that is reviewed before the next is started. This style suits the project for two practical reasons: understanding of the existing system grew as the work went on, and small, frequently-tested changes carry far less risk than large ones attempted all at once. The enhanced platform shall enable a customer to:

3.5 System Architecture

The architecture of FanMidCommerce is best understood as a set of layers stacked on top of one another, each with a clear job and a clean boundary to the layer below. At the top is the presentation layer, built with the Next.js App Router. Here, server components are used for the data-heavy pages that need to be fast and findable by search engines, such as product and category pages, while client components handle the parts that must respond instantly to the user, such as the cart and checkout. Below the presentation layer sits the API layer: a single GraphQL endpoint, served by Apollo Server through a Next.js route handler, which acts as the one controlled doorway through which the front end asks for and changes data. Beneath this, a domain/service layer encapsulates business logic, for example, server-authoritative pricing and order finalisation, while a data-access layer, built on Mongoose, mediates interaction with MongoDB. External integrations for payment (Paystack), email (Resend), and media (Cloudinary) are isolated behind dedicated service modules, so that they may be reconfigured or substituted per deployment. A central design principle is the separation of a reusable “commerce engine” (authentication, products, cart, checkout, orders, payments, and the administrative panel) from a customisable presentation layer (theme palettes, homepage section composition, and branding configuration). This separation supports the reusability objective: a single, tested core may be re-skinned for different businesses by editing configuration and selecting a theme, without modifying the underlying logic. It also operationalises the compatibility and reduced-complexity attributes that, per Diffusion of Innovations theory (Rogers, 2003), favour adoption.

3.6 Database Design

Because MongoDB stores data as flexible documents rather than rigid tables, the design of the data model is largely a series of decisions about how to shape those documents and how to connect them. In this study the model is expressed as a set of MongoDB collections, each defined through a Mongoose schema. Most relationships between collections are handled by reference, that is, one document stores the identifier of another, much as a foreign key works in a relational database. In a few cases, however, data is embedded directly inside its parent because it is naturally owned by that parent and is almost always read with it: the line items of an order live inside the order document, and a customer's saved addresses live inside the user document. 3.7 Application Programming Interface (API) Design

The API is defined as a GraphQL schema composed of types, queries, and mutations, modularised by domain (for example, products, orders, users, and content) and merged into a single executable schema. Queries provide read access, such as a paginated, filtered product listing and a product lookup by slug, while mutations perform state changes, including registration, login, payment verification and order creation, review submission, and administrative create, update, and delete operations.

A central technical concern, identified in Chapters One and Two, is the construction of the per-request GraphQL context within the Next.js App Router. The route handler that serves the GraphQL endpoint extracts the bearer token from the incoming request’s authorisation header, verifies the token’s signature on the server, and places the resulting authenticated-user information (identity and role) into the context object supplied to every resolver. Resolvers that perform privileged operations consult this context to enforce role-based authorisation before acting. This arrangement ensures that access control is enforced at the server boundary, independently of the state of the client interface, thereby resolving the context-propagation challenge that motivated the study. 3.8 User Interface and Experience Design

Interface design follows a mobile-first, responsive approach implemented with Tailwind CSS, with a theming system built on CSS custom properties and managed through next-themes to support light and dark modes and interchangeable colour palettes. Design decisions are guided by Nielsen’s (1994) usability heuristics, for instance, providing immediate feedback on actions such as adding an item to the cart, preventing errors at checkout through input validation, maintaining consistency across pages, and keeping the system’s status visible, and by the accessibility guidance of WCAG 2.1 (W3C, 2018) concerning contrast, keyboard operability, and semantic structure. The homepage is composed from configurable, reusable sections, enabling different businesses to present distinct layouts while reusing the same components, in line with the reusability objective and with TAM’s emphasis on perceived ease of use as a determinant of adoption (Davis, 1989).

3.9 Security Design

In a system that handles money and personal data, security cannot be a feature added at the end; it has to run through every layer. The design here follows the guidance of the OWASP (2021) project, and a few decisions deserve to be spelled out. Passwords are never stored in readable form, only as salted hashes, so that even a database breach does not expose them. User sessions are carried in signed JSON Web Tokens whose validity is always checked on the server, never merely trusted from the browser. The administrative and customer areas are protected at the server boundary itself, which means a privileged route or operation cannot be reached by an unauthorised client no matter what the front end happens to display; the interface might hide a button, but it is the server that actually refuses the action. Critically, checkout pricing is recomputed on the server rather than trusted from the client, and payment outcomes are verified directly with the payment provider; order finalisation is made idempotent and race-safe through a uniqueness constraint on the payment reference, preventing duplicate orders when concurrent confirmation paths occur, for example, when both a redirect callback and an asynchronous provider notification attempt to finalise the same payment. Inputs are validated, rate limiting is applied to public endpoints to mitigate abuse, and interactions with external services are isolated and handled defensively so that the failure of one does not compromise the others. Collectively, these measures address the access-control, cryptographic, and integrity risks emphasised in contemporary web-application security guidance.

3.11 Testing and Evaluation Strategy

The enhanced system is evaluated along the dimensions defined by the non-functional requirements, using the instruments summarised in Table 3.5. Functional correctness is assessed through unit testing of discrete logic, integration testing of API operations against the database, and user-acceptance testing of complete workflows such as browsing, checkout, and order management. Performance is examined through page-load and rendering metrics and the responsiveness of paginated data access. Usability is evaluated against Nielsen’s (1994) heuristics and, where feasible, through observation of representative users completing key tasks, optionally supplemented by a standard instrument such as the System Usability Scale. Accessibility is checked against WCAG 2.1 (W3C, 2018) using automated tooling and manual inspection. Security is assessed through a structured review informed by the OWASP (2021) guidance, covering authentication, authorisation, input handling, and payment integrity. Continuous type checking and production-build verification provide a baseline quality gate throughout development.
