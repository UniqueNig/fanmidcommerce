ABSTRACT

The expansion of internet and mobile connectivity in Nigeria has created a substantial digital marketplace, yet many small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) remain excluded from formal e-commerce by the cost of proprietary platforms, limited technical capacity, and the poor contextual fit of generic international solutions. At the same time, although modern web technologies, server-rendered React frameworks, statically typed JavaScript, and application programming interface (API)-first designs such as GraphQL, offer significant advantages in performance, maintainability, and developer productivity, the academic literature contains few integrated, end-to-end accounts of building a production-oriented e-commerce system on such a stack. This study addresses both gaps through the design, enhancement, and documentation of FanMidCommerce, a full-stack e-commerce web application built with Next.js (App Router), TypeScript, GraphQL with Apollo Server, and the MongoDB document database, and supported by the Paystack payment gateway and the Resend transactional-email service. Adopting a design science research methodology combined with an iterative, agile-inspired development process, the study analyses the existing codebase, enhances it with product search and filtering, order-management workflows, payment integration, and customer reviews, and resolves a recognised technical challenge concerning the propagation of authentication context to GraphQL resolvers within the Next.js App Router. The enhanced platform is evaluated for functionality, performance, usability, and security using established instruments, including Nielsen’s usability heuristics, the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines, and the OWASP security guidance. The study contributes a documented, reusable reference implementation for SME-oriented e-commerce, an analysis of GraphQL–Next.js integration suitable for researchers and practitioners, and an assessment of the platform’s applicability within the Nigerian digital economy. Chapters One to Three, presented here, cover the introduction, the review of related literature, and the research methodology and system design.

Keywords: e-commerce; full-stack web development; Next.js; GraphQL; MongoDB; TypeScript; design science; small and medium-sized enterprises; Nigeria.

Chapter One

Introduction

1.1 Background to the Study

The diffusion of internet and mobile technologies has profoundly reshaped commerce across the developing world, and nowhere is this transformation more consequential than in sub-Saharan Africa. Improvements in mobile broadband coverage, the proliferation of affordable smartphones, and steadily falling data costs have brought a large share of the population online, creating digital marketplaces of considerable scale and potential (International Telecommunication Union [ITU], 2023; GSMA, 2023). In Nigeria, the continent’s most populous nation and largest economy, internet penetration reached approximately 55.4 per cent of the population by early 2023, with broadband penetration standing at about 44.4 per cent by the end of 2024 (Nigerian Communications Commission [NCC], 2024). These developments have established the foundations for a rapidly maturing electronic-commerce sector that increasingly mediates the discovery, marketing, and exchange of goods and services.

Electronic commerce (e-commerce) denotes the buying and selling of goods and services, and the associated transfer of funds and data, conducted over electronic networks, principally the internet (Laudon & Traver, 2021). A complete e-commerce platform, however, extends well beyond the act of transacting. It integrates product-catalogue management, customer-account services, shopping-cart and checkout workflows, payment processing, order fulfilment and tracking, and post-sale communication into a single coherent system. The quality with which these components are designed and woven together materially influences conversion rates, customer retention, and the trust upon which repeat commerce depends (Laudon & Traver, 2021). For enterprises of all sizes, a capable platform offers reach beyond geographical boundaries and the ability to trade continuously, without the overheads of additional physical premises.

Micro, small and medium-sized enterprises (MSMEs) occupy a central position in the Nigerian economy. The most recent national survey jointly conducted by the Small and Medium Enterprises Development Agency of Nigeria and the National Bureau of Statistics enumerated approximately 39 million MSMEs, which together accounted for about 46.3 per cent of gross domestic product, some 87.9 per cent of national employment, and roughly 96.9 per cent of all businesses (National Bureau of Statistics & SMEDAN, 2021). For these enterprises, e-commerce represents a pathway to wider markets and greater resilience. Yet the adoption of formal e-commerce among Nigerian SMEs remains uneven. The barriers are well documented in broad terms: the recurring cost of proprietary, subscription-based platforms; limited in-house technical capacity to deploy and maintain web applications; concerns about payment security and consumer trust; and logistics constraints relating to delivery and returns (World Bank, 2019). A further, frequently overlooked barrier is contextual fit. Generic international platforms are not always adapted to local realities such as naira-denominated pricing, the prominence of bank-transfer and Unstructured Supplementary Service Data (USSD) payment channels alongside card payments, and the prevalence of informal ordering coordinated through messaging applications. The combined effect of these barriers is that many capable businesses remain confined to ad-hoc digital presences, typically a social-media page and manual order-taking, that lack the structure, security, and scalability of a dedicated platform.

In parallel with these market dynamics, the technologies used to construct web applications have advanced markedly. Contemporary practice increasingly favours full-stack JavaScript and TypeScript architectures, in which a single language and type system span both client and server; server-rendered React frameworks such as Next.js, which combine the interactivity of single-page applications with the performance and search-engine visibility of server rendering; and API styles such as GraphQL, which allow clients to request precisely the data they require from a strongly typed schema (Wieruch, 2022; Vercel, 2024). These approaches promise improved performance, stronger reliability through static typing, and a more productive developer experience than the legacy stacks on which many existing platforms are built. Despite their growing adoption in industry, however, there is comparatively little peer-reviewed documentation of their combined, end-to-end use in a complete, production-oriented e-commerce application, and even less that connects such technical work to the contextual requirements of an SME market such as Nigeria’s.

It is at the intersection of these two concerns, an under-served SME market and an under-documented modern technology stack, that the present study is situated. It concerns the design, enhancement, and rigorous documentation of FanMidCommerce, a full-stack e-commerce web application developed with Next.js (using the App Router), TypeScript, GraphQL served through Apollo Server, the MongoDB document database accessed through the Mongoose object-data-modelling library, and the Tailwind CSS styling framework, with payment and email capabilities provided by the Paystack and Resend services respectively. In its current form, the application already provides a customer-facing storefront, a customer account dashboard, and an administrative management panel. The study extends this foundation with additional commerce capabilities, resolves identified technical challenges, most notably the propagation of authentication context to GraphQL resolvers within the App Router, and produces a thorough account of the architectural and design decisions involved. In doing so, it advances both an academic contribution, in the form of a documented case study and reference implementation, and a practical one, in the form of a low-cost, locally contextualised, and reusable platform model for Nigerian SMEs.

1.2 Statement of the Problem

The global e-commerce sector has expanded rapidly, but the development of technically sound, feature-complete, and well-documented platforms built on modern JavaScript frameworks and API-first architectures remains comparatively under-explored in scholarly work. Four interrelated problems motivate this study.

First, a reliance on legacy architectures. A substantial proportion of available e-commerce solutions are built on older technology stacks that do not exploit the rendering performance, type safety, and maintainability advantages offered by modern frameworks such as Next.js with the App Router paradigm. Consequently, practitioners wishing to adopt these newer approaches have few documented, end-to-end blueprints to follow, and academic treatments tend to examine individual techniques in isolation rather than as parts of an integrated system.

Second, the difficulty of GraphQL integration within the Next.js App Router. Serving a GraphQL API from within a Next.js App Router route handler introduces particular technical difficulties. Chief among these is the reliable construction and propagation of authentication and authorisation context: the per-request information, such as the identity and role of the requesting user, that resolvers depend upon to enforce access control. This concern is fundamental to application security, yet it is addressed only thinly in the current literature, leaving developers to rediscover solutions independently.

Third, a scarcity of end-to-end development documentation. There is a limited body of work that documents the complete development lifecycle of a full-stack e-commerce application, from database schema design, through API specification, to user-interface implementation, using a TypeScript-first methodology. Such integrated accounts are valuable for teaching, for guiding industry practice, and for establishing reproducible patterns, but they are uncommon in the peer-reviewed literature.

Fourth, the treatment of usability and accessibility as afterthoughts. Accessibility, theming, and broader user-experience considerations are frequently appended late in academic prototypes rather than embedded as first-class requirements from the outset. This diminishes the practical value of such prototypes as genuinely deployable systems and overlooks the documented influence of usability and trust on e-commerce adoption.

A fifth, contextual problem compounds the preceding four within the study’s setting: even where capable platforms exist, they are frequently misaligned with the operational realities of Nigerian SMEs, particularly with respect to affordability, local payment channels, and owner-managed operation. Taken together, these problems indicate a clear need for an enhanced, thoroughly documented e-commerce platform that demonstrates modern architectural patterns, resolves the noted integration challenges, treats usability and accessibility as central design goals, and is deliberately fitted to the SME context. FanMidCommerce is the vehicle through which this study addresses that need.

1.3 Aim and Objectives of the Study

1.3.1 Aim

The aim of this study is to enhance and comprehensively document FanMidCommerce, a full-stack e-commerce web application, in order to demonstrate the practical application of modern web-development technologies and architectural patterns within an academic research framework, and to assess its suitability as an e-commerce solution for SMEs in the Nigerian context.

1.3.2 Objectives

The specific objectives of the study are to:

  1. review existing literature on e-commerce platform architectures, full-stack web-development frameworks, and API-driven application design;
  2. conduct a comprehensive analysis of the existing FanMidCommerce codebase in order to identify areas for functional and architectural enhancement;
  3. design and implement additional features, including product search and filtering, order-management workflows, payment-gateway integration, and customer review functionality;
  4. resolve identified technical challenges relating to GraphQL context management and authentication within the Next.js App Router environment;
  5. evaluate the performance, usability, and security of the enhanced application using appropriate assessment frameworks; and
  6. produce thorough technical documentation covering the system architecture, database schema, API design, and deployment procedures, and assess the applicability of the platform model for Nigerian SMEs.

1.4 Research Questions

Consistent with the objectives, the study is guided by the following research questions:

  1. How can a full-stack e-commerce platform be designed and enhanced using Next.js, TypeScript, GraphQL, and MongoDB to satisfy the core commerce requirements of SMEs?
  2. What are the principal technical challenges of integrating GraphQL authentication context within the Next.js App Router, and how may they be resolved?
  3. To what extent does the enhanced FanMidCommerce platform satisfy accepted criteria for functionality, performance, usability, and security?
  4. How applicable is the resulting platform model as an affordable and deployable e-commerce solution for SMEs within the Nigerian digital economy?

1.5 Significance of the Study

The study offers value to several audiences. For the academic community, it contributes a documented, real-world case study of full-stack e-commerce development on a modern TypeScript-first stack, an area in which peer-reviewed literature remains sparse. The detailed treatment of authentication-context propagation across the Next.js App Router GraphQL boundary addresses a specific and recognised gap, and the study’s structured evaluation provides a model for assessing comparable systems.

For practitioners and the open-source ecosystem, the enhanced platform constitutes a potentially deployable solution for small businesses seeking an online presence at low cost. Because the architecture separates a reusable commerce engine from a customisable presentation layer, a single, well-tested core can be adapted to multiple businesses with distinct visual identities, reducing the time and expense of delivering bespoke stores. The accompanying documentation lowers the barrier for other developers and students to understand, deploy, and extend such a system.

For the Nigerian SME sector, the study advances the broader objective of expanding accessible digital-commerce tooling adapted to local conditions, naira pricing, locally prevalent payment channels, and owner-managed operation, thereby supporting the digital inclusion of small enterprises. For students and educators, finally, the work serves as a reference implementation that illustrates how contemporary web technologies combine into a cohesive, production-oriented architecture, complementing fragmented tutorial material with an integrated, academically framed account.

1.6 Scope and Limitations of the Study

1.6.1 Scope

The study encompasses the enhancement and documentation of FanMidCommerce in its entirety, including the customer storefront, the customer account dashboard, and the administrative panel. The technology scope is bounded by the existing stack: Next.js (App Router) with TypeScript, GraphQL served through Apollo Server, MongoDB accessed via the Mongoose library, and Tailwind CSS for styling, together with the Paystack payment gateway, the Resend transactional-email service, and the Cloudinary media service. The research comprises a literature review, an analysis of the existing system, iterative design and implementation, and testing and evaluation. Functionally, it covers catalogue browsing with search, filtering, and pagination; product variants and stock management; customer authentication and account management; cart and secure checkout; payment integration; order management and notification; customer reviews; and administrative management of the foregoing.

1.6.2 Limitations

Several limitations are acknowledged. First, the platform is deployed to a development or staging environment for evaluation; large-scale, real-world transactional load testing lies beyond the present scope, so claims regarding behaviour under heavy concurrent load are necessarily provisional. Second, payment-gateway integration is exercised in sandbox/test mode, in keeping with the regulatory requirements governing live financial transactions; the live-payment configuration is documented but not transacted against. Third, usability evaluation is conducted with a limited number of representative participants rather than a large sample, which constrains statistical generalisation. Fourth, the findings are contextualised within the Nigerian digital landscape and may require adaptation before they generalise to other settings. These limitations qualify the strength of certain claims but do not diminish the study’s central contributions of design, implementation, and documentation.

2.2 Conceptual Review

2.2.1 Electronic Commerce and Its Models

Electronic commerce encompasses the range of commercial activities conducted over digital networks, including online retailing, electronic payment, and the supporting flows of information between buyers, sellers, and intermediaries (Laudon & Traver, 2021). It is commonly classified by the parties involved: business-to-consumer (B2C), in which enterprises sell directly to individual consumers; business-to-business (B2B); consumer-to-consumer (C2C), typically mediated by a marketplace; and consumer-to-business (C2B). FanMidCommerce is principally a B2C platform, designed to enable a single enterprise to sell directly to its customers. A functional B2C system integrates a product catalogue, a customer-account subsystem, a shopping cart and checkout process, a payment mechanism, an order-management and fulfilment workflow, and channels for customer communication. The seamlessness with which these elements operate, and the trust and perceived ease of use they engender, strongly influences conversion, retention, and ultimately commercial success (Laudon & Traver, 2021).

2.2.2 Full-Stack Web Development and TypeScript

Full-stack web development refers to the construction of both the client-facing interface and the server-side logic, together with the data layer that persists application state. The contemporary JavaScript ecosystem has made it possible to use a single language across the entire stack, reducing cognitive context-switching and enabling code and data structures to be shared between client and server (Mikowski & Powell, 2013). TypeScript, a statically typed superset of JavaScript, strengthens this model by introducing a type system that surfaces many classes of error at compile time rather than at run time, thereby improving reliability and maintainability, qualities of particular importance in a transactional system where defects can have financial consequences. The benefit is not merely theoretical: in a large-scale empirical study, Gao, Bird, and Barr (2017) added type annotations to code containing known, publicly reported JavaScript defects and found that a static type checker would have detected approximately 15 per cent of those bugs before release, providing concrete evidence that static typing prevents a meaningful share of real-world faults. The enduring value of clear module boundaries and consistent data contracts across the client–server boundary (Mikowski & Powell, 2013) further informs the layered, modular architecture adopted in this study.

2.2.3 The Next.js Framework and Rendering Strategies

Next.js is a React-based framework that provides server-side rendering (SSR), static site generation (SSG), incremental static regeneration (ISR), file-system routing, and an integrated build pipeline (Vercel, 2024). Its App Router model introduces a directory-based routing convention and a distinction between server components, which render on the server and may access back-end resources directly, and client components, which provide interactivity within the browser. The availability of multiple rendering strategies is significant for e-commerce: server rendering and static generation improve initial load performance and search-engine visibility, both of which affect discoverability and conversion, while client components support the interactive elements of cart and checkout. This separation, however, requires careful reasoning about where data fetching and authentication take place, a theme that recurs throughout the study. Search-engine optimisation, in particular, benefits from the server-rendered, crawlable markup that the framework produces for catalogue and product pages.

2.2.4 API Architectures: REST and GraphQL

An application programming interface (API) mediates the exchange of data between client and server. Representational State Transfer (REST), articulated by Fielding (2000), structures an API around resources accessed through a uniform interface, with each endpoint returning a fixed representation. While widely adopted and well understood, REST can lead to over-fetching (returning more data than the client needs) and under-fetching (necessitating multiple requests to assemble a view). GraphQL, by contrast, is a query language and runtime in which a typed schema defines the data a client may request, and the client specifies precisely the fields it requires (GraphQL Foundation, 2021; Wieruch, 2022). This client-driven model mitigates over- and under-fetching and provides a strongly typed contract that aligns naturally with a TypeScript codebase. Apollo Server is a widely used GraphQL implementation that supports schema definition, resolver composition, and a per-request context object through which shared information, notably the authenticated user, is supplied to resolvers (Apollo GraphQL, 2024). The reliable construction of this context within a Next.js App Router route handler constitutes a specific technical concern of the present study, and is treated in detail in Chapter Three.

2.2.5 Data Storage: Relational and Document Databases

Persistent storage may be provided by relational (SQL) databases, which organise data into fixed-schema tables related by keys, or by non-relational (NoSQL) databases, of which the document model is one variety. MongoDB is a document-oriented database that stores data as flexible, JSON-like documents rather than as rows in rigid tables (MongoDB, Inc., 2024). This model is well suited to the varied and evolving data structures common in e-commerce, for example, products that may or may not carry sizes, colours, or multiple images, and supports rapid iteration during development. The flexibility of the document model nonetheless places greater responsibility on the developer for disciplined data modelling, including deliberate decisions about embedding related data within a parent document versus referencing it across documents, and for appropriate indexing to preserve query performance as data grows. In this study, MongoDB is accessed through Mongoose, which layers schemas, validation, and a typed interface over the underlying database.

2.2.6 Authentication, Authorisation, and Security

Authentication establishes a user’s identity, whereas authorisation determines the actions that user is permitted to perform. JSON Web Tokens (JWTs) offer a common mechanism for stateless authentication, encoding signed claims that a server can verify on each request without maintaining server-side session state. In an application that separates customer and administrative areas, authorisation must be enforced consistently at the server boundary, so that privileged operations cannot be invoked by unauthorised clients irrespective of the state of the user interface. The Open Web Application Security Project (OWASP, 2021) identifies broken access control and cryptographic failures among the foremost categories of web-application risk; its guidance, together with the practice of recomputing sensitive values such as prices on the server and verifying payment outcomes with the provider, informs the security design presented in Chapter Three.

2.2.7 Online Payment Integration

Payment is the point at which an e-commerce transaction is consummated, and its integrity and convenience are decisive for consumer trust. Payment gateways abstract the complexity and regulatory burden of handling card and bank data by tokenising sensitive details and processing transactions on the merchant’s behalf. In the Nigerian context, locally established gateways such as Paystack support not only card payments but also bank transfer and USSD, channels that are widely used and, for many consumers, more trusted than card entry. Secure integration requires that the merchant application initiate transactions against the gateway, redirect or collect payment through the gateway’s secure interface, and independently verify the outcome with the gateway before fulfilling an order, rather than trusting any value reported by the client. These practices, together with idempotent order finalisation to prevent duplicate processing, are central to the design described later.

2.2.8 Theming, Usability, and Accessibility

The presentation layer determines, in large measure, whether a platform is adopted and trusted. Tailwind CSS is a utility-first styling framework that promotes consistent, responsive interfaces, while libraries such as next-themes enable dynamic light/dark theming through CSS custom properties. Beyond aesthetics, established usability heuristics (Nielsen, 1994), including visibility of system status, error prevention and recovery, consistency, and a match between the system and the real world, provide criteria against which an interface can be both designed and evaluated. Accessibility, codified in the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (W3C, 2018), adds requirements such as sufficient colour contrast, keyboard operability, and semantic structure, ensuring usability by people with diverse abilities. Treating these criteria as first-class requirements, embedded throughout development rather than appended at the end, is a stated commitment of this study and a direct response to a gap identified in Section 1.2.

2.3 Theoretical Framework

The study is underpinned by a framework that combines a methodological theory for constructing and evaluating the artefact with two adoption theories that explain its intended uptake, supported by foundational software-engineering principles.

2.3.1 Design Science Research

The principal methodological lens is Design Science Research (DSR), which holds that knowledge can be produced through the construction and rigorous evaluation of innovative artefacts that address identified problems (Hevner, March, Park, & Ram, 2004). Hevner et al. (2004) propose guidelines requiring that the research produce a viable artefact, that the artefact address a relevant problem, that it be evaluated rigorously, and that the work make a clear contribution and be communicated effectively. Peffers, Tuunanen, Rothenberger, and Chatterjee (2007) operationalise this through a Design Science Research Methodology (DSRM) comprising problem identification, definition of objectives for a solution, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication. This study maps directly onto these stages: the problem and objectives are established in Chapter One, the design and development are detailed in Chapter Three and realised in Chapter Four, and demonstration, evaluation, and communication follow.

2.3.2 The Technology Acceptance Model

To frame the platform’s intended adoption by SME operators and their customers, the study draws on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which posits that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are the primary determinants of a user’s intention to adopt a technology (Davis, 1989). TAM provides a rationale for the study’s emphasis on usability and accessibility: a platform that is perceived as difficult to use is unlikely to be adopted, however technically capable it may be. The model thus links the interface-design objectives to the broader goal of practical applicability.

2.3.3 Diffusion of Innovations

Complementing TAM, Rogers’ (2003) Diffusion of Innovations theory explains how innovations spread through a social system and identifies attributes that influence the rate of adoption, including relative advantage, compatibility, complexity, trialability, and observability. These attributes are pertinent to the design choices made in this study: the separation of a reusable engine from a customisable presentation layer improves compatibility and reduces the effective complexity of deploying new stores, while low cost and local contextual fit enhance relative advantage for SMEs.

2.3.4 Software-Engineering Principles

Finally, the construction of the artefact is governed by long-standing software-engineering principles , modularity, separation of concerns, abstraction, and information hiding, that promote maintainable and extensible systems (Sommerville, 2016; Pressman & Maxim, 2020). These principles are expressed concretely in the layered architecture and in the deliberate division between commerce logic and presentation described in Chapter Three.

2.4 E-Commerce and SMEs in the Nigerian Context

Nigeria presents a large, youthful, and increasingly connected market, yet one in which SME adoption of formal e-commerce remains uneven (ITU, 2023; GSMA, 2023; World Bank, 2019). The scale of both the opportunity and the enterprise base is considerable: internet penetration reached roughly 55.4 per cent of the population by early 2023, with broadband penetration at about 44.4 per cent by the close of 2024 (NCC, 2024), while the national MSME survey enumerated some 39 million micro, small and medium-sized enterprises contributing approximately 46.3 per cent of gross domestic product and 87.9 per cent of employment (National Bureau of Statistics & SMEDAN, 2021). Despite this, formal e-commerce adoption among these enterprises remains limited, indicating a persistent gap between connectivity and commercial digitalisation. Several contextual factors shape the requirements for a platform intended to serve this market. First, connectivity is predominantly mobile: most users access the internet through smartphones over mobile networks, which makes a mobile-first, performance-conscious interface essential rather than optional. Second, payment behaviour is heterogeneous: alongside card payments, bank transfers and USSD-based transactions are widely used and, for many consumers, more familiar and more trusted; a platform that supports only card entry therefore excludes a meaningful segment of potential buyers. Third, trust and informality coexist: a significant volume of trade is coordinated through social media and messaging applications, and consumers often prefer to confirm details through such channels before or instead of a formal checkout. Fourth, cost sensitivity is acute: recurring platform fees weigh heavily on small traders, favouring solutions with low or one-off costs.

The barriers to adoption reported in the literature align closely with these factors. In a study of SME operators across Nigeria’s six geo-political zones, Awa, Awara, and Lebari (2015) identified critical inhibitors including weak firm finances, firm size, non-transparency in government support, location factors, and concerns over the confidentiality of information. Agwu and Murray (2015), in an empirical investigation of small and medium-scale businesses, similarly found that the absence of a robust regulatory and security framework for e-commerce, limited technical skills, and inadequate basic infrastructure were principal barriers to adoption. These findings echo broader assessments that cite the cost of proprietary platforms, limited technical capacity, concerns over payment security and trust, and logistics constraints relating to delivery and returns (World Bank, 2019). A platform designed for this market must therefore accommodate naira-denominated pricing; support card, transfer, and USSD payment channels; bridge formal checkout and messaging-based enquiry; and be operable by a non-technical owner after minimal training. These contextual requirements are carried forward directly into the requirements analysis of Chapter Three.

2.5 Empirical Review of Related Studies

Beyond the conceptual and contextual literature, a growing body of empirical research has tested, under controlled or comparative conditions, the specific technologies and adoption questions on which this study rests. This section reviews that evidence across eight strands: the comparative performance of API architectures (REST versus GraphQL); the performance and search-engine implications of server-rendered React frameworks such as Next.js; the comparative behaviour of document and relational databases; the empirical determinants of e-commerce adoption in developing economies and Nigeria; the effect of static typing on software quality; web-application security and authentication; the relationship between usability, trust, and purchase intention; and digital-payment adoption in Nigeria. Each study is summarised in terms of what was done, what was found, and what limitations bear on the present work.

2.5.1 Empirical Studies on REST and GraphQL

The choice of GraphQL over REST in this study is supported by a maturing experimental literature. In a frequently cited controlled experiment, Brito and Valente (2020) tasked twenty-two participants with implementing eight queries using both REST and GraphQL and found that GraphQL significantly reduced the effort required to construct remote-service queries, with lower median completion times than REST. In earlier work, Brito, Mombach, and Valente (2019) conducted a practical assessment of migrating REST APIs to GraphQL and reported that GraphQL could substantially reduce the size of JSON responses returned to clients by eliminating over-fetching, although the migration itself demanded careful schema design. Complementary measurement studies have examined run-time behaviour: Seabra, Nazário, and Pinto (2019) found that migrating from REST to GraphQL reduced latency in two of three tested applications, chiefly by reducing the number of network round-trips, although REST outperformed GraphQL under very high request volumes (above roughly three thousand requests). This indicates that GraphQL’s data-transfer benefits are most pronounced for complex, nested queries and moderate loads. The evidence is not uniformly in GraphQL’s favour, however; several performance evaluations note that REST can retain advantages in raw response time and memory efficiency under certain high-load, simple-query conditions. The balance of findings indicates that GraphQL’s principal benefits, precise data selection, fewer round trips, and a strongly typed contract, are most valuable for data-rich, client-diverse applications such as e-commerce storefronts, which is the rationale for its adoption here.

2.5.2 Empirical Studies on Next.js and Server-Side Rendering

The performance and discoverability advantages claimed for server-rendered React frameworks are likewise supported empirically. Pati and Zaki (2025) conducted a comparative analysis of Next.js and a conventional client-rendered React application, measuring metrics including First Contentful Paint and Time to Interactive across varied network and device conditions; they reported that Next.js delivered better overall performance without degrading interaction quality, an effect attributable to server rendering and automated optimisation. Studies focused on search-engine optimisation report a similar pattern: because server rendering delivers fully formed HTML that crawlers can index directly, Next.js applications tend to achieve markedly higher automated SEO scores than equivalent client-rendered React applications (Pati & Zaki, 2025; industry SEO analyses). For an e-commerce platform, where product and category pages must be both fast and discoverable, this body of evidence directly motivates the use of Next.js server components for catalogue pages, as adopted in the present design.

2.5.3 Empirical Studies on Document and Relational Databases

The selection of MongoDB as the data store is informed by comparative database research. In a widely cited web-application study, Gyorodi, Gyorodi, Pecherle, and Olah (2015) benchmarked MongoDB against MySQL across the core create, read, update, and delete operations and found that MongoDB achieved faster performance on several operations, especially bulk insertion of large datasets, attributing the advantage to the document model and the absence of rigid joins. Broader systematic comparisons temper this picture: relational systems retain advantages for complex, multi-table queries and for workloads demanding strict transactional (ACID) consistency, whereas document databases favour flexible schemas and horizontal scalability (Győrödi et al., 2015; systematic reviews of database performance). For an e-commerce catalogue characterised by heterogeneous, evolving product structures, such as items that may or may not carry sizes, colours, or multiple images, the flexibility of the document model is advantageous, provided that disciplined data modelling and indexing are applied, as discussed in Chapter Three.

2.5.4 Empirical Studies on E-Commerce Adoption in Developing Economies and Nigeria

A substantial empirical literature applies adoption theory, particularly the Technology Acceptance Model and its extensions, to online shopping in developing economies, consistently finding that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use shape adoption intentions, alongside context-specific factors such as trust, social influence, and facilitating conditions. Within the Nigerian context specifically, Usman and Kumar (2021) examined the factors influencing consumers’ intention to shop online and emphasised the decisive roles of attitude, trust, and perceived usefulness and ease of use in enhancing the acceptability of online shopping. On the enterprise side, Awa, Awara, and Lebari (2015) identified weak finances, firm size, location, and confidentiality concerns as critical inhibitors of e-commerce adoption among Nigerian SMEs, while Agwu and Murray (2015) highlighted the absence of robust regulatory and security frameworks, limited technical skills, and inadequate infrastructure. A recurring and instructive finding is that Nigerian consumers frequently perceive e-commerce as useful and easy to use yet fail to complete transactions, owing to weak facilitating conditions, including trust, payment, and logistics. This evidence directly motivates several design priorities of the present study: support for locally trusted payment channels, server-side payment verification and security to build trust, and a usable, accessible interface intended to convert favourable perceptions into completed purchases.

2.5.5 Empirical Studies on Static Typing and Software Quality

Because reliability is a stated non-functional requirement of this study, the empirical evidence on static typing merits separate attention. The landmark study by Gao, Bird, and Barr (2017) examined real, publicly reported defects in JavaScript projects and demonstrated that a static type system would have caught roughly 15 per cent of them prior to release, a substantial proportion for a single, low-cost intervention. Subsequent work has produced a more nuanced picture. Comparative analyses of open-source repositories report that TypeScript projects exhibit better code quality and readability than comparable JavaScript projects, although the effect on raw bug-proneness is smaller than often assumed: in a mining study of 604 GitHub projects, Bogner and Merkel (2022) found that TypeScript applications had significantly better code quality and understandability, yet using TypeScript did not automatically yield fewer or more easily fixed bugs. More recent ecosystem studies observe a "shifted fault boundary": as static typing suppresses traditional type and runtime errors, a larger share of remaining faults migrates to build tooling, configuration, and asynchronous error handling. For the present study, the implication is clear: TypeScript is adopted for its demonstrated quality and maintainability benefits, while testing and review remain necessary to address the classes of fault that typing alone does not eliminate.

2.5.6 Empirical Studies on Web Application Security and Authentication

The security design of this study is grounded in a substantial empirical and practitioner literature. The OWASP Top 10 (2021), itself derived from analysis of large volumes of real-world vulnerability data, ranks broken access control and cryptographic failures among the most prevalent and damaging categories of web-application risk. Within the specific area of token-based authentication, security analyses repeatedly identify a recurring and serious class of vulnerability: applications that merely decode a JSON Web Token rather than cryptographically verifying its signature, which can permit user impersonation and privilege escalation. This finding is directly pertinent to the architecture proposed here, in which a fast, decode-only check may be used at the network edge for routing convenience but authoritative signature verification is always performed on the server before any privileged action. Comparative evaluations of security-testing tooling further indicate that no single automated tool detects all vulnerability classes, supporting this study’s use of a structured, OWASP-informed review rather than reliance on a single scanner.

2.5.7 Empirical Studies on Usability, Trust, and Purchase Intention

A consistent empirical literature links website usability and trust to the intention to purchase, which is the ultimate measure of an e-commerce platform’s effectiveness. Studies measuring the influence of usability attributes on purchase intention find that trustworthiness ranks among the most important factors, alongside ease of comprehension and shopping support, and that usability can itself be increased by enhancing trust through clear information, responsive customer care, and visual clarity. Validated instruments, including purpose-built online-shop usability scales and the widely used System Usability Scale, have been employed to quantify these effects, with regression analyses indicating that usability exerts the greatest single influence on buying intention. This evidence substantiates the study’s treatment of usability and accessibility as first-class requirements and informs the choice of evaluation instruments described in Chapter Three.

2.5.8 Empirical Studies on Digital Payment Adoption in Nigeria

Because payment is the point at which an e-commerce transaction succeeds or fails, the empirical literature on digital-payment adoption in Nigeria is especially relevant. Studies applying the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT) to mobile money and mobile payment in Nigeria find that performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions shape adoption intentions, and that trust and network effects are particularly influential in the Nigerian context. Multi-country evidence from sub-Saharan Africa further identifies education, bank-account ownership, and mobile-phone access as determinants of adoption. Taken together with the consumer-behaviour finding that Nigerian shoppers often perceive e-commerce favourably yet abandon transactions because of weak facilitating conditions, this literature reinforces a central design decision of the study: to integrate a locally trusted payment gateway supporting card, bank-transfer, and USSD channels, and to verify payment outcomes on the server so as to build the trust that the evidence shows is decisive for transaction completion.

2.6 Review of Related Systems

Existing e-commerce solutions can be grouped into hosted platforms, self-hosted open-source systems, modern headless/API-first frameworks, and bespoke builds. Hosted platforms such as Shopify provide rapid setup and managed infrastructure but entail recurring subscription costs and limited control over the underlying code and data, constraints that are significant for cost-sensitive SMEs. Self-hosted open-source systems such as WooCommerce (built on WordPress and PHP) and Magento offer greater control and extensibility but are rooted in older technology stacks and can impose considerable maintenance, performance, and hosting overheads. Modern headless and API-first frameworks such as Medusa and Saleor decouple the storefront from the commerce engine and embrace contemporary tooling, improving flexibility, but they introduce integration complexity and a steeper learning curve that may exceed the capacity of a small business or a solo developer. Bespoke builds afford complete control and contextual fit but, without a reusable foundation, must be re-created for each client at considerable cost.

Within the scholarly and practitioner literature, accounts of building commerce systems on modern React/Next.js and GraphQL foundations tend to address isolated concerns, a particular rendering strategy, a single API pattern, or a discrete feature, rather than documenting an integrated, end-to-end system that also attends to authentication, security, theming, accessibility, and contextual fit. Studies of SME e-commerce adoption in Nigeria, conversely, emphasise economic, infrastructural, and regulatory barriers (Awa et al., 2015; Agwu & Murray, 2015) but seldom connect those findings to concrete architectural choices in a deployable platform. 2.7 Summary of Literature and Research Gap

The literature reviewed establishes the strategic value of e-commerce for SMEs, the technical advantages of modern full-stack and API-first architectures, and the central importance of usability, accessibility, and security to adoption and trust. It also confirms the contextual particularities of the Nigerian market, mobile-first access, heterogeneous payment channels, the interplay of formal and informal ordering, and acute cost sensitivity. Against this backdrop, three gaps are evident. First, there is a scarcity of integrated, end-to-end, and well-documented accounts of building a production-oriented e-commerce application on a Next.js, TypeScript, GraphQL, and MongoDB stack. Second, the specific challenge of authentication-context propagation within the Next.js App Router GraphQL endpoint is inadequately addressed. Third, few works connect modern architectural practice to the concrete requirements of SME e-commerce in a market such as Nigeria’s. This study addresses these gaps through the design, enhancement, documentation, and evaluation of FanMidCommerce, as set out in the methodology that follows.

Chapter Three

Research Methodology and System Design

3.1 Introduction

This chapter describes the methodology adopted for the study and the design of the enhanced FanMidCommerce platform. It presents the research design, the software-development methodology, the requirements analysis, and the architectural, database, API, interface, and security designs, before detailing the tools employed and the strategy for testing and evaluation. The approach is design-and- development oriented: the principal research output is a working, documented software artefact, evaluated against defined functional and non-functional criteria, consistent with the design science research paradigm introduced in Chapter Two.

3.2 Research Design

The study adopts a design science research (DSR) design, in which knowledge is generated through the construction and evaluation of a purposeful artefact, here, the enhanced FanMidCommerce platform (Hevner et al., 2004; Peffers et al., 2007). This is complemented by a descriptive case-study element, since a single platform is examined in depth to surface design decisions, challenges, and their resolutions. The design is mixed-method: qualitative evidence (architectural analysis, documented design rationale, and usability observation) is combined with quantitative evidence (performance measurements and test outcomes). This orientation is appropriate because the research questions concern how a system can be designed and built, and how well it performs, rather than the testing of hypotheses about a population. The six activities of the Design Science Research Methodology, problem identification, objective definition, design and development, demonstration, evaluation, and communication (Peffers et al., 2007), provide the overarching structure within which the development methodology operates.

3.3 Software Development Methodology

Within the DSR design, the construction work follows an iterative, agile-inspired development methodology (Beck et al., 2001; Sommerville, 2016). Rather than a single linear pass, enhancement proceeds in short cycles, each delivering a working increment that is reviewed before the next begins; this accommodates evolving understanding and reduces the risk associated with large, untested changes. 3.4 Requirements Analysis

Requirements were derived from the analysis of the existing system, the literature, and the contextual factors identified in Chapter Two. They are presented as functional requirements, non-functional requirements, and the principal use cases.

3.4.1 Functional Requirements

The enhanced platform shall enable a customer to:

  • browse products by category, with keyword search, attribute filtering (price, size, and others), and server-side pagination;
  • view rich product detail, including multiple images, size and colour variants, and per-variant stock, with clear out-of-stock indication;
  • register, authenticate, and manage a profile and saved delivery addresses;
  • add items to a cart and complete a secure checkout in which totals are computed on the server and validated discount codes and shipping options are applied;
  • pay online through an integrated gateway supporting card, bank transfer, and USSD, with the outcome verified on the server; and
  • submit reviews of purchased products and view those of others.

The platform shall enable an administrator to:

  • manage products, categories, discount codes, and shipping methods;
  • view and process orders through defined status stages, triggering customer notifications;
  • manage customers, reviews, and site content; and
  • operate under role-based access control that distinguishes administrators from customers.

3.5 System Architecture

FanMidCommerce follows a layered, modular architecture. The presentation layer is implemented with the Next.js App Router, using server components for data-driven, search-indexable pages (such as product and category pages) and client components for interactive elements (such as the cart and checkout). The application exposes a single GraphQL endpoint, served by Apollo Server through a Next.js route handler, which constitutes the API layer. Beneath this, a domain/service layer encapsulates business logic, for example, server-authoritative pricing and order finalisation, while a data-access layer, built on Mongoose, mediates interaction with MongoDB. External integrations for payment (Paystack), email (Resend), and media (Cloudinary) are isolated behind dedicated service modules, so that they may be reconfigured or substituted per deployment. A central design principle is the separation of a reusable “commerce engine” (authentication, products, cart, checkout, orders, payments, and the administrative panel) from a customisable presentation layer (theme palettes, homepage section composition, and branding configuration). This separation supports the reusability objective: a single, tested core may be re-skinned for different businesses by editing configuration and selecting a theme, without modifying the underlying logic. It also operationalises the compatibility and reduced-complexity attributes that, per Diffusion of Innovations theory (Rogers, 2003), favour adoption.

3.7 Application Programming Interface (API) Design

The API is defined as a GraphQL schema composed of types, queries, and mutations, modularised by domain (for example, products, orders, users, and content) and merged into a single executable schema. Queries provide read access, such as a paginated, filtered product listing and a product lookup by slug, while mutations perform state changes, including registration, login, payment verification and order creation, review submission, and administrative create, update, and delete operations.

A central technical concern, identified in Chapters One and Two, is the construction of the per-request GraphQL context within the Next.js App Router. The route handler that serves the GraphQL endpoint extracts the bearer token from the incoming request’s authorisation header, verifies the token’s signature on the server, and places the resulting authenticated-user information (identity and role) into the context object supplied to every resolver. Resolvers that perform privileged operations consult this context to enforce role-based authorisation before acting. This arrangement ensures that access control is enforced at the server boundary, independently of the state of the client interface, thereby resolving the context-propagation challenge that motivated the study. The end-to-end flow of an authenticated, payment-bearing request is illustrated for the checkout case in Figure 3.4.

3.8 User Interface and Experience Design

Interface design follows a mobile-first, responsive approach implemented with Tailwind CSS, with a theming system built on CSS custom properties and managed through next-themes to support light and dark modes and interchangeable colour palettes. Design decisions are guided by Nielsen’s (1994) usability heuristics, for instance, providing immediate feedback on actions such as adding an item to the cart, preventing errors at checkout through input validation, maintaining consistency across pages, and keeping the system’s status visible, and by the accessibility guidance of WCAG 2.1 (W3C, 2018) concerning contrast, keyboard operability, and semantic structure. The homepage is composed from configurable, reusable sections, enabling different businesses to present distinct layouts while reusing the same components, in line with the reusability objective and with TAM’s emphasis on perceived ease of use as a determinant of adoption (Davis, 1989).

3.9 Security Design

Security is treated as a cross-cutting requirement informed by the OWASP (2021) guidance. Passwords are stored solely as salted hashes; sessions are conveyed through signed JSON Web Tokens whose validity is checked on the server. Administrative and customer areas are protected at the server boundary, so that privileged routes and operations remain inaccessible to unauthorised clients regardless of client-side state. Critically, checkout pricing is recomputed on the server rather than trusted from the client, and payment outcomes are verified directly with the payment provider; order finalisation is made idempotent and race-safe through a uniqueness constraint on the payment reference, preventing duplicate orders when concurrent confirmation paths occur, for example, when both a redirect callback and an asynchronous provider notification attempt to finalise the same payment. Inputs are validated, rate limiting is applied to public endpoints to mitigate abuse, and interactions with external services are isolated and handled defensively so that the failure of one does not compromise the others. Collectively, these measures address the access-control, cryptographic, and integrity risks emphasised in contemporary web-application security guidance.
